Each actor has exactly **one mailbox** — a FIFO queue of envelopes
waiting to be processed.  When you call `ref.tell(message)`, the
framework wraps `message` in an envelope (with sender, log-context, and
optional trace context) and enqueues it on the recipient's mailbox.
The dispatcher pulls the next envelope, hands it to the actor's
`onReceive`, and waits for that to finish before pulling the next
one.

That gives every actor the "one message at a time" guarantee — and
the mailbox is the thing that makes that physically true.

## The default mailbox

If you don't configure anything, the actor gets an **unbounded FIFO
mailbox**.  Nothing is ever discarded on the way in; the queue grows
until the actor drains it, and the heap is the only ceiling.

Why unbounded?  Because the alternative is worse in a way that is easy
to miss.  Between v0.10 and v0.15 the default was bounded — 10 000
messages, `drop-head` — and the framework silently discarded the
*oldest* queued message whenever an actor fell behind.  The trade was
supposed to be "lose some messages, gain a memory ceiling", and it did
not hold up:

- **The ceiling was not real.**  Only the *user* queue was bounded.
  System messages — lifecycle, supervision, watch — were never capped,
  so a node could still exhaust its heap.
- **A mailbox cannot tell what it is discarding.**  `drop-head` suits
  telemetry, where the freshest reading is the only one that matters.
  It does not suit a delivery confirmation or a WebSocket `close` — and
  those went through the same queue.  Each of those turned into a filed
  defect.

  Three messages are now out of every policy's reach: the death-watch
  `Terminated`, the command that spawns a
  [WebSocket](/http/websocket/#bounding-the-hubs-mailbox) connection's
  actor, and that connection's own `close`.  All three take a lane no
  bound can shed (see [Death watch](/fundamentals/death-watch/) and
  *A mailbox of your own* below).  The test they pass is that each is
  sent once with nothing left to re-send it from, and that losing one
  costs you not a *message* but a piece of state outside the mailbox
  that nothing else will correct — a watcher that thinks a dead actor
  is alive, an upgraded socket nobody attached to, a peer you decided
  to disconnect and did not.  The rest is still application traffic,
  and only you know which of it is safe to lose.

So the loss is now something you ask for — per actor at the spawn site,
or once for a whole deployment through
`actor-ts.mailbox.default.capacity`, which bounds every actor your
application wrote — sharded entities and cluster singletons included,
even though the framework spawns those — and leaves the framework's own
actors alone.
That key ships as `0`, meaning off, and turning it on is the same
decision as bounding one actor, taken for all of them at once: see
[Mailbox sizing](/operations/tuning/mailbox-sizing/) for what it does
and does not reach.

The growth, meanwhile, is something you are told about: an actor whose queue reaches 10 000
messages logs a warning, and again at each doubling.  With metrics
enabled, `actor_mailbox_size` reports the depth per actor above that
mark, and `actor_mailbox_depth` reports the whole distribution below it —
so a burst that never reaches the warning threshold is still visible.

A queue with no ceiling has to stay cheap when it is deep, so both of a
mailbox's queues are backed by a [`RingBuffer`](/reference/utility-helpers/)
rather than a plain array: taking the next message advances an index instead
of reindexing everything behind it, so the cost of delivering one message
does not grow with the backlog waiting behind it.  This is invisible from
the outside — including to a `Mailbox` subclass of your own, which sees only
the methods.

### When to bound a mailbox

Reach for a bound when **shedding load is better than absorbing it**,
and you can say which messages are safe to lose:

- A **telemetry or sensor sink** where only the newest reading matters.
  `drop-head` keeps the queue fresh.
- An **admission point** in front of an expensive pipeline, where you
  would rather refuse work than queue it.  `drop-new` keeps what you
  already accepted, `reject` tells the sender to back off.
- Any actor exposed to a producer you do not control, where an
  unbounded queue is a denial-of-service surface.  Read the third bullet
  with the first two: it says where a bound is *worth* considering, not
  that the traffic there is expendable.  A WebSocket hub is the clearest
  case of both at once — see
  [Bounding the hub's mailbox](/http/websocket/#bounding-the-hubs-mailbox)
  for what a bound there does and does not cost.

```ts
import { ActorOptions } from 'actor-ts';

const sensorOptions = ActorOptions.create()
  .withMailboxCapacity(10_000)
  .withMailboxOverflow('drop-head');

system.spawn(SensorSink, 'sensors', sensorOptions);
```

Setting a capacity is what creates the bound; `withMailboxOverflow`
decides which message is lost, and defaults to `drop-head`.  Setting a
policy without a capacity is rejected rather than silently ignored — an
unbounded mailbox never overflows, so it would have nothing to do.  That
rejection stands even where `actor-ts.mailbox.default.capacity` supplies
a bound: the validator runs at the spawn call and knows nothing about
the system the actor will be spawned into, so restate the capacity
alongside the policy when you mean to reshape a global bound for one
actor.

Both fields have the same config layer under them —
`actor-ts.mailbox.default.capacity` and `.overflow`.  The spawn site
always wins; the block decides what an actor that said nothing gets.

The three policies and how to choose between them are covered under
[`BoundedMailbox`](#boundedmailbox) below — including why `reject` fails
the *sender* rather than the slow actor.

Drops from a capacity you set are counted by
`actor_mailbox_dropped_total`, labelled with the actor's class and the
policy that fired.  Per class, not per actor: shedding is what a bounded
mailbox is *for*, so a per-actor label would mint a permanent metric
series for every actor behaving exactly as designed.  To see which
instance is shedding, add an `onDrop` of your own — it runs alongside the
stock counter rather than replacing it, and it is handed the envelope that
was discarded, so it can say what was lost and not only that something
was.

To keep that envelope rather than inspect it in passing, build the mailbox
with `deadLetterDrops: true` and each dropped message becomes a
[dead letter](/operations/dead-letters/) with its sender intact.  It is
off by default, and only reachable from a mailbox you construct yourself:
the drop runs on the sender's stack, so routing every shed envelope is
work charged to exactly the burst the bound was meant to absorb cheaply.
See [mailbox sizing](/operations/tuning/mailbox-sizing/#keeping-what-you-dropped).

### Bringing your own mailbox

`withMailbox` replaces the queue outright — for a `PriorityMailbox`, a
`ControlAwareMailbox`, a `BoundedMailbox` configured beyond what the two
options above express, or a subclass of your own:

```ts
import { ActorOptions, PriorityMailbox } from 'actor-ts';

const triageOptions = ActorOptions.create()
  .withMailbox(() => new PriorityMailbox({ priorityFor: (m) => m.urgency }));

system.spawn(Triage, 'triage', triageOptions);
```

The mailbox is yours, but its drops are still counted: the cell registers
an observer on whatever you return, provided it implements
`DropReportingMailbox` — `BoundedMailbox` does, and a `Mailbox` subclass of
your own can by adding one method:

```ts
import { Mailbox, type Envelope, type MailboxDropObserver } from 'actor-ts';

class SheddingMailbox<T> extends Mailbox<T> {
  private readonly observers: Array<MailboxDropObserver<T>> = [];
  readonly deadLetterDrops = true;

  observeDrops(observer: MailboxDropObserver<T>): void {
    this.observers.push(observer);
  }

  override enqueue(envelope: Envelope<T>): void {
    if (this.shouldShed()) {
      for (const observer of this.observers) observer('drop-new', envelope);
      return;
    }
    super.enqueue(envelope);
  }
}
```

A report carries the envelope as well as the reason, and `deadLetterDrops`
says whether the cell should turn each one into a
[dead letter](/operations/dead-letters/).  Hand over the envelope you
actually discarded — under a `drop-head`-shaped policy that is the one you
evicted, not the one that arrived, or every letter will name a message that
is still queued.  Both are optional: omit the field and the drop is counted
and nothing more, which is what `BoundedMailbox` does unless you ask
otherwise.

Registration is additive, so a `BoundedMailboxOptions.onDrop` of your own
keeps firing alongside the stock counter.  What you cannot do is combine
`withMailbox` with `withMailboxCapacity` — that is a configuration error
rather than a silent precedence rule.

#### One obligation: `enqueueSignal`

A queue that sheds has a short list of things it must not shed: the four
envelopes the framework sends exactly once, with nothing left to send them
again from.  A death-watch `Terminated` — see
[Death watch](/fundamentals/death-watch/) — comes straight from the dying
actor for a local death, and after one hop on the system queue for a death
another node reported; a WebSocket hub gets the command that spawns a
connection's actor; a connection's actor gets its own `close`; and an
actor subscribed to a [DistributedData](/distributed-data/overview/) key
gets the `DistributedDataChanged` that tells it the key's latest value.
All of them arrive through `enqueueSignal` rather than `enqueue`, and the
contract is *queue it whatever your bound says*:

```ts
class SheddingMailbox<T> extends Mailbox<T> {
  // ...as above...

  /** Past the bound, deliberately: this one cannot be re-sent. */
  override enqueueSignal(envelope: Envelope<T>): void {
    super.enqueue(envelope);
  }
}
```

The base implementation delegates to your `enqueue`, which is correct for
a queue that discards nothing and wrong for one that does — so **override
it whenever you override `enqueue` to shed**.  `BoundedMailbox`,
`PriorityMailbox` and `ControlAwareMailbox` already do.  Delegating rather
than writing straight to the base queue is deliberate: a subclass may keep
its messages somewhere else entirely, and an envelope hidden in a store
your `dequeueUser` never reads is worse than one you dropped.

The envelope also carries `undroppable: true`, which `removeOldest` reads
— so a `drop-head`-shaped bound built on that seam steps over it for
free, and does not have to know why.  If your `enqueue` throws instead,
the framework turns the refusal into a
[dead letter](/operations/dead-letters/) and the dying actor's teardown
continues; what it cannot do is invent a second copy of the
notification.  A refused `DistributedDataChanged` is logged by the
replicator instead, and the next change to the key tries again.

The `DistributedDataChanged` is the newest of the four, and it is on the
list for the reason the others are rather than for its subject.  The
replicator keeps at most one notification per subscriber and key in
flight: while one waits in your queue, later changes overwrite its value
in place, and the next one is posted only once your actor has been handed
it.  Shed it, and it is never handed over — the replicator goes on
waiting, every later change lands in a message nobody reads, and the actor
is not told about that key again.  What it costs your bound is at most one
envelope per subscribed key, and it keeps its place at the tail while its
value is overwritten.

**Undroppable is not queue-jumping.**  The door decides that a signal
survives your bound, never where it lands: queue it where you would queue
any other message, which on a FIFO queue is the tail.  Two of the four
depend on that — death watch promises that everything already told to the
watcher is handled before the `Terminated`, and a reply queued before a
WebSocket `close()` is written before the socket shuts.  A queue that
orders messages by its own rule orders signals by the same rule:
`PriorityMailbox` ranks a `Terminated` with your `priorityFor`.  And a
queue that should let some messages overtake the rest says so by
classifying them, the way [`ControlAwareMailbox`](#controlawaremailbox)
does — never by moving whatever came through this door to the front.

## System messages always come first

Inside every mailbox, two queues live side-by-side: **user messages**
(your `tell`s) and **system messages** (lifecycle signals — create,
terminate, failure, watch, …).  System messages have **absolute
precedence**: even if 10 000 user messages are queued, the next
`stop` signal or supervisor `failure` is processed before any of
them.

This matters because:

- Calling `ref.stop()` does **not** jump the queue — under the
  hood it's a *user* message (`PoisonPill`), so the actor first
  drains the user messages already queued ahead of it, then stops
  (a graceful drain-then-stop).  Only framework-emitted system
  messages get the absolute precedence above.
- A failing actor's supervisor decision (Restart / Resume / Stop)
  takes effect immediately, not after the queue clears.
- A death-watch `Terminated` is a *user* message too, for the same
  reason `PoisonPill` is: it must arrive **after** everything already
  told to the watcher, so a handler that reacts to a death sees the
  work that preceded it.  Undroppable is not the same as
  queue-jumping — it keeps its place in line and merely cannot be
  discarded from it.  That holds for every signal the framework sends
  that way, not for this one alone: a message overtakes the user queue
  only on a mailbox that classifies messages, like
  [`ControlAwareMailbox`](#controlawaremailbox), and only when its
  predicate says so — yours, or on a WebSocket hub the framework's.

**A mailbox capacity bounds the user queue only.**  The system queue is
never capped — not by `BoundedMailbox`, not by a mailbox of your own —
because supervision and lifecycle signals must never be dropped.  Its
depth is bounded by what produces them instead: one `create` per actor
and one `terminate` per stop, one `childTerminated` per child that
finishes stopping, one `failure` per supervision decision plus the
`suspend` / `resume` / `recreate` that decision fans out over the
failing subtree, one `receiveTimeout` per armed timer that expires, and
one `watchNotify` per `Terminated` that arrives from *another node* — a
remote actor this node's own actors watched, or a node they held
watches into that left the cluster.  Every one of those was paid for by
an actor that had to exist first, or by a watch one of them registered.

A local death notification is **not** on that list.  A watched actor's
`Terminated` goes onto its watcher's *user* queue — past the bound, as
above, but still behind everything already told to that watcher — so
watching a thousand local actors adds nothing to the system queue at
all.  A `Terminated` reported by another node takes one system-queue
hop to become that same user-queue signal; a peer can send at most one
per watch this node opened, and an unsolicited one matches nothing.

You don't normally see this distinction — system messages are
emitted by the framework, not by your code.  But understanding it
explains why "supervision reacts instantly."

## `BoundedMailbox`

```ts
import { ActorOptions, Actor, ActorSystem, BoundedMailbox } from 'actor-ts';

class SlowConsumer extends Actor<{ kind: 'work'; n: number }> {
  override async onReceive(message: { kind: 'work'; n: number }): Promise<void> {
    await new Promise(r => setTimeout(r, 100));   // simulate slow work
    this.log.info(`processed ${message.n}`);
  }
}

const system = ActorSystem.create('demo');

const consumerOptions = ActorOptions.create()
  .withMailbox(() => new BoundedMailbox({ capacity: 1_000, overflow: 'drop-head' }));

const consumer = system.spawn(SlowConsumer, 'consumer', consumerOptions);
```

The mailbox here holds up to 1 000 user messages.  When a 1 001st
message arrives, the **overflow policy** decides what happens.

Three policies:

| Policy | What happens on overflow |
| --- | --- |
| `'drop-head'` | Dequeue the *oldest* message in the queue, discard it, enqueue the new one.  Newest messages always make it in. |
| `'drop-new'` | Discard the *incoming* message.  The old queue is preserved unchanged. |
| `'reject'` | Throw `MailboxFullError` at the `tell` site.  Caller surfaces the backpressure. |

Which one you get when you name none depends on which door you came
through, and the difference is deliberate: constructing a
`BoundedMailbox` yourself defaults to **`reject`**, because you built a
bound and nothing else can be assumed about what is safe to lose.
`withMailboxOverflow` defaults to **`drop-head`**, the policy that suits
the workloads people actually reach for a bound to protect.  Name the
policy either way and the question does not arise.

The options are validated at construction: a missing or non-positive
`capacity` and an unknown overflow policy throw `OptionsError`.

`capacity` bounds the messages the mailbox is allowed to *discard*, which
in a mailbox holding nothing but undelivered death notifications means the
queue sits above it rather than losing one.  The overshoot is bounded by
how many actors this one watches — and by how many DistributedData keys
it subscribes to, one notification each — and it is the price of the
exemption [Death watch](/fundamentals/death-watch/) describes: size a
capacity for your traffic, not to the byte.

Picking between them is a backpressure-vs-loss trade-off:

- **`drop-head`** = "freshest wins."  Right for telemetry, sensor
  data, status pings — where stale messages are worthless and the
  latest snapshot is the only thing that matters.
- **`drop-new`** = "first wins."  Right for command-streams where
  re-ordering is unacceptable and dropping a late arrival is OK.
- **`reject`** = "let the sender deal with it."  Right when the
  sender has a meaningful backoff response (retry, route to a
  different actor, return 503 from an HTTP handler).

`droppedCount` on the mailbox instance tracks how many messages
have been discarded — useful to wire into a metrics gauge so you
notice when the bound is hit.

import { Aside } from '@astrojs/starlight/components';

<Aside type="caution" title="`reject` and the calling actor">
  `reject` throws at the **`tell` call site**.  If actor A
  `tell`s B and B's mailbox is full with the `reject` policy,
  the throw lands inside A's `onReceive` — caught by A's
  supervisor, not B's.  That's usually fine (the supervisor for
  A decides how A handles the unreachability), but it means
  "B's mailbox is overflowing" surfaces as "A keeps restarting,"
  which can confuse triage.  Wire a metric on `droppedCount`
  rather than relying on supervisor logs to catch it.
</Aside>

## `PriorityMailbox`

```ts
import { ActorOptions, Actor, ActorSystem, PriorityMailbox } from 'actor-ts';

type Message =
  | { readonly kind: 'urgent'; readonly text: string }
  | { readonly kind: 'normal'; readonly text: string }
  | { readonly kind: 'bulk';   readonly text: string };

class Worker extends Actor<Message> {
  override onReceive(message: Message): void {
    this.log.info(`[${message.kind}] ${message.text}`);
  }
}

const workerOptions = ActorOptions.create<Message>()
  .withMailbox(() => new PriorityMailbox<Message>({
    priorityFor: (message) => message.kind === 'urgent' ? 0
                        : message.kind === 'normal' ? 5
                        : 10,
  }));

const worker = system.spawn(Worker, 'worker', workerOptions);

worker.tell({ kind: 'bulk',   text: 'batch import row 1' });
worker.tell({ kind: 'normal', text: 'user login' });
worker.tell({ kind: 'urgent', text: 'page-out: disk full' });
// → processed order: urgent → normal → bulk
```

The `priorityFor` callback runs at *enqueue* time, computing a
numeric priority per message.  **Lower numbers go first** (priority
0 is highest), and ties break by FIFO insertion order — so two
`'normal'` messages stay in send-order relative to each other.

Common shapes for `priorityFor`:

- **Per-`kind` constant table** — like the example above.  Easy to
  read, easy to evolve.
- **Field-derived** — `priorityFor: (m) => m.deadlineMs` makes
  earliest-deadline messages run first.  Works because both axes
  are "lower = sooner."
- **Caller-tagged** — sender includes `priority: number` in the
  message and `priorityFor` just reads it.  Sometimes the right
  call; usually a smell that the recipient should derive priority
  from message content instead.

<Aside type="caution" title="Priority mailboxes break FIFO across kinds">
  A `normal` message sent *after* an `urgent` one can still
  arrive at `onReceive` *first*.  If your invariants depend on
  "in the order it was sent," priority mailboxes aren't right
  — split into two actors instead, one per priority class, and
  funnel via a router.
</Aside>

### When `priorityFor` cannot answer

Your callback does not have to be total.  If it **throws**, or if it
answers with something that is not a usable number — `undefined`, `NaN`,
a value of another type — the message is queued at the **lowest**
priority and nothing else happens.  It is not dropped and the failure
does not surface at the `tell` site.

Both halves matter:

- **Lowest, not highest.**  A priority that could not be determined must
  not outrank one that was stated.  Ties still break by FIFO, so
  unrankable messages stay in send-order relative to each other.
- **Contained, not thrown.**  `priorityFor` runs synchronously inside the
  *sender's* `tell`.  A sender is a bystander to the receiver's mailbox
  configuration: it has nothing to do about a broken `priorityFor` and no
  way to tell that from a failure of its own.  An escaping throw landed
  in the sender's `onReceive` and got the **sender** restarted — the same
  confusion the `reject` caution above describes, for a bug that has
  nothing to do with the sender at all.

`±Infinity` is a *ranking*, not a failure: `-Infinity` means "ahead of
everything", `+Infinity` means "behind everything", and both are ordered
as written.  Only `NaN` and non-numbers count as undeterminable.

<Aside type="caution" title="The framework calls `priorityFor` with `PoisonPill` and `Kill`">
  `ref.stop()` and `ref.kill()` post `PoisonPill` / `Kill` as ordinary
  **user** messages — that is what makes `stop()` a graceful
  drain-then-stop rather than a queue-jump.  So `priorityFor` sees them
  too, and neither has any fields: a `match(message).…​.exhaustive()`
  throws on both, and the **field-derived** and **caller-tagged** shapes
  above return `undefined`.

  The containment means that is survivable rather than fatal, but rank
  them deliberately instead of relying on it — an `.otherwise(() => 5)`
  arm, or a `?? 5` on the field.  Otherwise every `stop()` costs you a
  contained failure and a mailbox entry at the very back of the queue.
</Aside>

To see a contained failure rather than only its consequences, pass
`onPriorityError`.  Nothing else in the system reports one — the message
still arrives, just last:

```ts
import { PriorityMailbox, PriorityMailboxOptions } from 'actor-ts';

const triageOptions = PriorityMailboxOptions.create<Message>()
  .withPriorityFor((message) => message.kind === 'urgent' ? 0 : 10)
  .withOnPriorityError((cause, message) => {
    log.warn(`priorityFor could not rank ${JSON.stringify(message)}: ${String(cause)}`);
  });

const mailbox = new PriorityMailbox<Message>(triageOptions);
```

`cause` is whatever `priorityFor` threw, or a `TypeError` describing the
value it returned.  **Record and return** from the hook: it runs on the
sender's stack too, so a throw from inside it puts the escape back — and
costs the message its place in the queue.

The current implementation uses a sorted-insertion array — O(log n)
locate + O(n) splice on each enqueue.  Fine for mailboxes that stay
in the low thousands; if you have a sustained 10 000-message backlog
where priority insertion shows up in profiles, the mailbox is open
to a heap-backed swap (see the source).

### Bounding a priority mailbox

A priority mailbox is unbounded unless you give it a `capacity`, exactly
like every other mailbox.  The bound lives on the mailbox's own options
rather than on `ActorOptions`, because `withMailbox` and
`withMailboxCapacity` cannot be combined — a supplied mailbox brings its
own bound:

```ts
import { ActorOptions, PriorityMailbox, PriorityMailboxOptions } from 'actor-ts';

const triageOptions = PriorityMailboxOptions.create<Message>()
  .withPriorityFor((message) => message.kind === 'urgent' ? 0 : 10)
  .withCapacity(10_000)
  .withOverflow('drop-lowest-priority');

const workerOptions = ActorOptions.create<Message>()
  .withMailbox(() => new PriorityMailbox<Message>(triageOptions));
```

The policies are `'drop-lowest-priority'`, `'drop-new'` and `'reject'`, and
`reject` is what you get when you name a capacity without one — naming a
capacity says how much you will hold, not what you are willing to lose.

| Policy | What happens on overflow |
| --- | --- |
| `'drop-lowest-priority'` | Discard the message furthest from delivery — the lowest-priority one, and among equals the one that arrived last.  The arriving message competes on the same terms, so a message ranked below the whole backlog is the one that goes. |
| `'drop-new'` | Discard the *incoming* message, whatever priority it was given. |
| `'reject'` | Throw `MailboxFullError` at the `tell` site. |

`drop-head` is deliberately **not** offered here.  On a FIFO queue it means
"discard the stalest", which works because arrival order is the only order
there is.  On a priority queue the head is the message your `priorityFor`
called most important — dropping it would defeat the reason you chose this
mailbox.  Drops are still reported as `drop-head` on
`actor_mailbox_dropped_total` (that label has a closed two-value
vocabulary), except when the discarded message was the arriving one, which
reports `drop-new`.

<Aside type="caution" title="A bounded mailbox can drop on unstash">
  `PriorityMailbox.prependUser` re-runs the priority function rather than
  pushing to the front, so `unstashAll()` goes through the same `enqueue`
  path — and therefore through the same bound.  On a full mailbox, replaying
  the stash can discard messages.  `BoundedMailbox` arrives at the same
  guarantee by a different route: the replay goes in at the head and the
  mailbox sheds at the *tail* to make room, so what a full queue costs is its
  newest messages rather than the ones you stashed.  Either way the bound
  applies to the replay — and under `reject`, `unstashAll()` throws
  `MailboxFullError` rather than dropping anything.
</Aside>

The options are validated at construction: a missing or non-callable
`priorityFor`, a non-positive `capacity`, an unknown policy, or a policy
without a capacity all throw `OptionsError`.  What the callback *returns*
is a runtime question and is handled at the enqueue instead — see
[When `priorityFor` cannot answer](#when-priorityfor-cannot-answer).  On a
bounded mailbox the two meet: an unrankable message is the least important
thing in the queue, so under `drop-lowest-priority` it is the arrival that
gets shed, reported as `drop-new`.

## `ControlAwareMailbox`

```ts
import { Actor, ActorOptions, ActorSystem, ControlAwareMailbox, ControlAwareMailboxOptions } from 'actor-ts';

type PauseCommand = { readonly kind: 'pause' };
type ResumeCommand = { readonly kind: 'resume' };
type ReadingCommand = { readonly kind: 'reading'; readonly value: number };
type IngestCommand = PauseCommand | ResumeCommand | ReadingCommand;

class Ingest extends Actor<IngestCommand> {
  override onReceive(command: IngestCommand): void {
    this.log.info(`handled ${command.kind}`);
  }
}

// Name the control kinds rather than excluding the rest — see the cautions below.
const isControl = (command: IngestCommand): boolean =>
  command.kind === 'pause' || command.kind === 'resume';

const ingestMailboxOptions = ControlAwareMailboxOptions.create<IngestCommand>()
  .withIsControl(isControl)
  .withCapacity(10_000)
  .withOverflow('drop-head');

const newIngestMailbox = () => new ControlAwareMailbox<IngestCommand>(ingestMailboxOptions);
const ingestOptions = ActorOptions.create<IngestCommand>().withMailbox(newIngestMailbox);

const system = ActorSystem.create('demo');
const ingest = system.spawn(Ingest, 'ingest', ingestOptions);

ingest.tell({ kind: 'reading', value: 1 });
ingest.tell({ kind: 'reading', value: 2 });
ingest.tell({ kind: 'pause' });
// → handled order: pause → reading → reading
```

A `ControlAwareMailbox` keeps two FIFO queues and hands the actor every
**control** message before any **ordinary** one, arrival order kept inside
each.  The predicate you pass as `isControl` decides which is which, once
per message, as it arrives.  Nothing else changes: system messages still
come first, and the framework classifies messages as control on its own
for one actor only — the WebSocket hub, below.

Reach for it when a few kinds of message must not wait behind a backlog of
the rest — a pause or a reconfiguration in front of a flood of readings, an
administrative command in front of a stream of data.  Where you want a
whole ordering by importance rather than two classes, a `PriorityMailbox`
is the tool; where everything has to stay in the order it was sent, use
neither.

### The WebSocket hub has one by default

A [`WebsocketServerActor`](/http/websocket/#bounding-the-hubs-mailbox)
gets a `ControlAwareMailbox` without being asked, unless it is spawned with
a mailbox of its own.  Its control messages are the command that hands it
an upgraded socket and the `onClientConnected` signal the new connection
reports; frames, disconnects and your own messages to the hub stay
ordinary.  A new connection is therefore set up ahead of every other
connection's backlog and is never shed by a bound, while the order per
connection — connected, messages, disconnected — stays as it was.  A
capacity the hub would otherwise have had, from `withMailboxCapacity()` or
`actor-ts.mailbox.default.capacity`, becomes the capacity of the ordinary
class.  `withMailbox(...)` replaces the queue, and the control lane with
it.

### Bounding a control-aware mailbox

A `capacity` bounds the **ordinary** class only, and the three
`BoundedMailbox` policies — `'drop-head'`, `'drop-new'`, `'reject'` — act
on it alone.  A control message is never shed, never refused, never
counted as a drop and never makes `reject` throw: the flood a bound sheds
is the traffic the control message is there to overtake.  So the capacity
is a ceiling on the ordinary class, and the control class is bounded by
whatever sends it — which is one more reason to keep the predicate to the
few kinds that need to overtake.  Named without a policy, a capacity means
`reject`, as on every mailbox you construct yourself.

`unstashAll()` puts each message back at the head of its own class, and
the ordinary half meets the bound the way a `BoundedMailbox` replay does.
Drops reach `actor_mailbox_dropped_total` and your `onDrop`, and
`deadLetterDrops: true` turns each one into a
[dead letter](/operations/dead-letters/).

The options are validated at construction: a missing or non-callable
`isControl`, a non-positive `capacity`, an unknown policy, and a policy or
`deadLetterDrops` without a capacity all throw `OptionsError`.

### When `isControl` cannot answer

`isControl` runs inside the **sender's** `tell`, so it is contained the
same way `priorityFor` is: if it throws, or answers anything but `true`,
the message is ordinary and the `tell` goes through.  Ordinary is not only
the safe answer but the right one — it is the class the message would have
had on any other mailbox.

<Aside type="caution" title="A control message overtakes its own sender's earlier messages">
  Control-first is a property of the queue, not of each sender.  A control
  message told *after* ordinary ones by the same sender is still handled
  first, so anything that relies on "in the order I sent it" across the two
  classes breaks.  Keep the control class to messages that make sense out of
  order, and leave anything whose meaning depends on what came before it
  ordinary.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="Name the control kinds — the framework's own messages reach the predicate">
  `ref.stop()` and `ref.kill()` post `PoisonPill` and `Kill` as ordinary
  user messages, and neither has a `kind`.  A predicate written as
  `command.kind !== 'reading'` answers `true` for both, so `stop()` would
  overtake the backlog instead of draining it.  Match the control kinds
  (`command.kind === 'pause'`) and everything else, the framework's messages
  included, stays ordinary.
</Aside>

<Aside type="note" title="Death watch keeps its order">
  A death-watch `Terminated` reaches this mailbox through the same exempt
  door as any other: past the bound, at the position the mailbox gives it.
  Here that is the tail of the ordinary class, behind everything already
  told to the watcher — unless your predicate classes the `Terminated`
  itself as control, in which case it overtakes the work that preceded the
  death.
</Aside>

## Per-actor mailbox via ActorOptions

Three knobs on `ActorOptions`:

```ts
import { ActorOptions, PriorityMailbox } from 'actor-ts';

// Bound the default FIFO, and say what a full one discards.
const cappedOptions = ActorOptions.create()
  .withMailboxCapacity(500)
  .withMailboxOverflow('drop-new');

// Full custom factory — pick the type and configure it.
const customOptions = ActorOptions.create()
  .withMailbox(() => new PriorityMailbox({ priorityFor: (m) => m.urgency }));
```

`withMailboxCapacity(n)` turns the default FIFO into a bounded one;
`withMailboxOverflow` picks the policy, defaulting to `drop-head`.  The
policy on its own is rejected — an unbounded mailbox never overflows, so
it would be a no-op that reads like configuration.

`withMailbox(factory)` is the general form — you return a brand-new
mailbox instance from the factory.  The factory is called once per
actor instance (including on restart), so each restarted actor
gets a fresh empty mailbox-data-structure.  It **replaces** the queue
rather than configuring it, so combining it with `withMailboxCapacity`
is a configuration error rather than a silent precedence rule, and the
framework wires no drop telemetry into whatever you return.

The default is unbounded: a bound is chosen per actor with these knobs,
or once for a whole deployment with `actor-ts.mailbox.default.capacity`,
which the spawn site's own setting always overrides — see
[Mailbox sizing](/operations/tuning/mailbox-sizing/) for the actors it
reaches.

<Aside type="caution" title="Don't share a mailbox instance across actors">
  ```ts
import { ActorOptions } from 'actor-ts';

  const shared = new BoundedMailbox({ capacity: 100 });
  const aOptions = ActorOptions.create().withMailbox(() => shared);
  const bOptions = ActorOptions.create().withMailbox(() => shared);  // wrong
  ```
  Each actor needs its **own** mailbox.  Returning the same
  instance from two factories pipes both actors' messages into
  one queue — they'll see each other's messages and the runtime
  gets confused about which actor is being dispatched.  The
  factory exists exactly so you create a fresh one each time.
</Aside>

## Mailboxes + stash

When an actor calls `this.context.stash()` inside `onReceive`, the
current message is parked.  When the actor later calls
`unstashAll()`, the parked messages are re-prepended to the front
of the mailbox.

This works the same way for every mailbox type — the framework
calls `mailbox.prependUser(envs)`, and the mailbox decides how to
reinsert.  Notably for `PriorityMailbox`, unstashed messages are
*re-prioritized*: a stashed `bulk` message rejoins the `bulk` tier,
even if you stashed it while urgent messages were arriving.  Stash
order is preserved within a priority tier.  A `ControlAwareMailbox`
puts each message back at the head of its own class, so a stashed
control message is still handed over before the ordinary ones.

**A bound applies to the replay.**  Whichever bounded mailbox you
chose, `unstashAll()` is subject to the capacity and the overflow
policy you set — a replay is traffic re-entering the queue, not a
back door around it.  What differs is only where the room comes
from: `PriorityMailbox` re-ranks, and `BoundedMailbox` puts the
replay at the head and sheds at the tail, so a full queue loses its
newest messages rather than the ones you deliberately parked; a
`ControlAwareMailbox` does the same over its ordinary class.  Under
`reject` nothing is lost at all — `unstashAll()` throws
`MailboxFullError`, the whole batch stays stashed, and the failure
reaches your actor's supervisor like any other.  Size the capacity
for the backlog *plus* whatever you expect to stash, or accept the
drops as the price of the bound.

**One exception, and it is the same one as everywhere else.**  An
envelope marked `undroppable` — a death-watch `Terminated`, a WebSocket
accept or close command, a DistributedData change notification — is
admitted past the bound on the way back in too.  The marker travels on the envelope rather than being remembered
by the queue, precisely so it survives a `context.stash()` /
`unstashAll()` round trip: a bound gets one chance to shed a
notification, not a second one because the actor parked it first.

**That is a claim about the bound, not about what the actor will
see.**  A `Terminated` parked under a plain `watch` is admitted here
and then refused by the dispatch, because the registration the death
consumed is the thing that would have admitted it — so it ends as a
[dead letter](/operations/dead-letters/) rather than a second
delivery.  Which is not the bound's doing, and is exactly why the two
are worth keeping apart: a notification the bound shed never reaches
the dispatch at all, so it leaves no dead letter behind.  See
[Death watch](/fundamentals/death-watch/) for the registration that
does survive a stash — `watchWith`, whose domain message the death
watch has no further opinion about.  The WebSocket accept and close
commands carry no such bookkeeping and are redelivered normally, and so
is a DistributedData change notification — the replicator stopped
overwriting it the moment the actor was first handed it, so it replays
with the value it had then.

<Aside type="caution" title="The typed stash does not carry the marker yet">
  That exception covers `context.stash()` — the untyped stash, which
  parks the envelope the actor is handling and replays the same object.
  The typed DSL's `Behaviors.withStash` cannot: `stash.stash(message)`
  takes any value, so its buffer holds bare messages and the replay
  rebuilds envelopes from them.  Everything the envelope carried is
  gone by then, `undroppable` included.

  So a **typed** actor with both a `withStash` behavior and a bounded
  mailbox can still lose a stashed `Terminated` to its own bound.  Until
  that is closed, give such an actor an unbounded mailbox, or unstash
  before the queue can fill.  Nothing about the untyped path changes,
  and neither path is affected while the mailbox is unbounded — which is
  the default.
</Aside>

See [Become and stash](/fundamentals/become-and-stash/) for
the full behavior-switching story.

## When the mailbox choice matters

For most actors, the default unbounded FIFO is right.  Reach for an
alternative in four situations:

1. **Producer/consumer mismatch.**  The producer can emit faster
   than the consumer can drain.  Bound the consumer's mailbox;
   pick an overflow policy that matches the workload (drop-head
   for telemetry, reject for HTTP-driven backpressure).
2. **Latency budget per kind.**  Some messages must be handled in
   tens of milliseconds (user-facing requests), others can wait
   minutes (background reconciliation).  Priority mailbox; the
   urgent kind gets `0`, the background kind gets `100`.
3. **Memory bound.**  An actor with no application-level priority
   distinction whose queue will grow without limit if it falls behind
   — an audit-log subscriber during a spike, or anything fed by a
   producer you do not control.  Bound it at a number that matches your
   memory budget; `drop-head` if the latest events are most valuable,
   `drop-new` if the ones you already accepted are.  Watch
   `actor_mailbox_wait_seconds` to find out whether you need this
   before you need it — it climbs as soon as an actor stops keeping up,
   where `actor_mailbox_size` only reports once a queue passes 10 000
   messages.  `actor_mailbox_depth`'s p99 is what to pick the number
   itself from: a capacity is a bet on the worst-case burst, and that
   histogram is the measurement of it.
4. **A command stuck behind a backlog.**  A few kinds of message — a
   pause, a reconfiguration, an administrative request — must not wait
   behind a flood of everything else, and the rest has no ranking worth
   paying a priority mailbox for.  `ControlAwareMailbox`; name those
   kinds in its predicate.
